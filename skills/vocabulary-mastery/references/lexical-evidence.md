# Lexical evidence and uncertainty: conditional reference

Read when the target has ambiguous senses, pronunciation, homographs, regional variation, controversial derivation, unusual collocations, or is coined/unattested.

## Decision process

1. **Respect context first.** Separate what the user's sentence indicates from what is known independently about the lexical item. If context resolves a sense, prioritize it in both languages.
2. **Separate senses, word classes, and pronunciations.** Homographs may differ in pronunciation, inflection, syntax or meaning. Align every sense with the relevant pronunciation before writing an example.
3. **Rank evidence conservatively.** Prefer dictionary-verified IPA, established grammatical descriptions and attested usage when actually accessible. If no lookup was performed, don't imply that an entry, corpus count or audio exists. For an ordinary well-known form, provide a qualified reference analysis when confidence is high; otherwise mark uncertainty or omit specific IPA/frequency/region details.
4. **Distinguish derivation from resemblance.** Segmentation into prefixes, stems and suffixes requires a real productive or historically supported analysis. Do not invent etymologies from surface spelling.
5. **Support collocations and frequency claims.** Give familiar conventional examples when reliably known; avoid exaggerated statements like 'always', 'never', 'extremely frequent', or fabricated statistical rankings.
6. **Handle coinages honestly.** A user-created word is not evidence of standard pronunciation, dictionary meaning, usage frequency, conventional collocations or origin. Separate any user-defined sense from general claims and label illustrative suggestions as hypothetical.
7. **Match both languages.** Repeat the same sense-IPA caveats, exclusions and confidence limits in English and Chinese. Do not fill missing evidence with confident Chinese paraphrases.

## Diagnostic examples (not required output text)

- **record**: noun and verb commonly have different lexical stress; keep sense, word class and stress aligned when presenting reference IPA.
- **lead**: distinguish the guiding verb pronounced approximately /liːd/ from the metal noun /led/; do not assign a single IPA to all homographs.
- **bank** in a riverbank sentence: prioritize the edge-of-river sense in both references, even if a financial-institution sense is also mentioned.
- **look up** in dictionary-search sense: grammar must distinguish 'look it up' from nonstandard 'look up it' for the separable particle use.
- **invented word**: mark conventional pronunciation, etymology and established usage as unknown; only describe a hypothetical proposed analysis as such.

## What to report

When evidence does not justify a specific factual detail, explain the gap briefly and show an honest illustration of the ordinary linguistic dimension. Reference IPA and sentence-level prosodic examples are not claims about a user's produced speech.
