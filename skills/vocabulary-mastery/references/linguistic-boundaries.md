# Linguistic boundary distinctions: conditional reference

Read when a target makes closely related linguistic dimensions easy to conflate, or when a word has no distinctive property in a required section.

| Domain | Unit of explanation | Boundary and failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phonetics | Realizable speech segments/articulation | Reference description is not assessment of the learner's actual sounds |
| Phonology | Language-level sound patterns, syllables, lexical stress | Word stress is distinct from sentence prominence |
| Morphology | Word structure and inflection/derivation | A spelling resemblance is not evidence of an affix or etymology |
| Lexicon | Conventional combinations/lexical relations | Avoid presenting invented collocations as established |
| Syntax | Grammatical role and permitted constructions | Separate meaning from the structural distribution of a word |
| Semantics | Encoded senses and compositional interpretation | Preserve polysemy/homographs and contextual distinctions |
| Pragmatics | Speaker intention, register and social context | Not every word has a unique speech act or politeness function |
| Discourse | Coherence, reference, links across turns or sentences | A single isolated clause may not show discourse function |
| Information Structure | Given/new information, topic/focus in context | Show the preceding question or conversational context |
| Prosody | Rhythm, intonation, sentence-level prominence | Illustrative sentence melody is not an observed recording |

## Illustrating weak domains without fabrication

When there is no recognized special word-specific property:

1. Explicitly say so in each language.
2. Explain the *general mechanism* in a genuine example involving the word; label the example contextual or illustrative rather than a special property of the word.
3. Preserve the same English example sentence and add its Chinese rendering in the second reference.
4. Avoid long, generic filler added solely to satisfy the ten headings.

Example: *apple* has no intrinsic sentence intonation pattern or unique speech act. A question-response about which fruit was bought can illustrate **information focus**, while a possible emphatic spoken form can illustrate **prosody**, without falsely describing them as dictionary properties of *apple*.

## Knowledge connections

Connections should explain causal/functional relationships: a chosen sense constrains a syntactic frame, a verb-noun distinction may affect lexical stress, and information focus changes where speakers might place sentence prominence. Do not conflate all ten categories into a list of synonyms.
